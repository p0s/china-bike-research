## Nominal sizes do not translate between brands

A “51,” “52,” or “M” can have very different stack, reach, head angle, seat angle, and cockpit dimensions. Start from a bike whose position you know, then compare coordinates.

Record saddle height, saddle setback, horizontal distance from saddle reference point to hood, hood height relative to saddle, bar width, stem length, crank length, and preferred spacer stack.

## Separate frame and cockpit reach

Frame reach is only part of the position. Add stem length, handlebar reach, hood shape, and hood placement. Integrated cockpits often have non-standard effective lengths and forward sweep. A frame that looks shorter can produce a longer hood position.

## Stack is not bar height

Frame stack, headset top cover, spacers, stem angle, bar rise, and hood shape all affect hand height. Check maximum permitted spacer height and whether the steerer arrives pre-cut.

## Choose replaceable fit risk

When between sizes, ask which error is easier to correct. A slightly short conventional cockpit may be easy to lengthen. An overly long proprietary integrated cockpit can be expensive or unavailable. Toe overlap, standover, handling, and seatpost exposure also matter, so “always size down” and “always size up” are both bad universal rules.

## Require a full geometry chart

Do not buy from a seller who provides only rider-height ranges. Ask for stack, reach, effective top tube, head/seat angles, head tube, chainstay, bottom-bracket drop, fork offset, wheelbase, standover, and the exact included cockpit width/length for every size.

## Compare with a small spreadsheet

The repository keeps geometry out of the main ranking until reliable size-by-size data is available. For a purchase, build a one-row comparison against your current bike and calculate approximate hood coordinates. A correct fit is more valuable than a marginal carbon-grade or weight claim.
