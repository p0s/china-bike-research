## Fiber grade is not frame quality

Labels such as T700, T800, T1000, T1100, M40, or M46J describe only part of a material system. Good frames mix fibers and orientations according to local loads. Higher-modulus fibers can improve stiffness at lower weight, but they can also be less tolerant of impact and require careful design.

A frame does not become better merely because a listing prints the largest number.

## What matters more

- ply orientation, continuity, and local reinforcement;
- controlled resin content;
- compaction and removal of wrinkles, voids, and resin-rich areas;
- cure temperature and repeatability;
- bond quality at dropouts, inserts, and other interfaces;
- head-tube, bottom-bracket, brake-mount, and axle alignment;
- fatigue, overload, and impact margins;
- batch traceability and serial control;
- inspection and warranty response.

EPS, bladder molding, one-piece/monocoque claims, or smooth internal walls can be useful signals, but marketing terminology is not a substitute for model-specific process and test evidence.

## Separate expectation from confidence

This guide deliberately uses two concepts:

- **Expected execution**: the best evidence-based estimate of how well the frame is likely to be designed and made.
- **Evidence confidence**: how much public information supports that estimate.

A low-cost factory-direct frame may have good expected execution but only medium or low confidence. Missing information widens the uncertainty interval; it does not prove poor quality.

## Factory ownership is useful, not decisive

An integrated manufacturer can control equipment, training, traceability, and feedback. A design brand using a competent contract factory can also produce an excellent frame. Conversely, owning a factory does not guarantee every unit is aligned.

Ask a narrower question: who is responsible for the exact model, how is production controlled, and who will remedy a bad unit?

## Beware ultra-light claims without context

A very light gravel frame may be genuinely well engineered. It also gives you more reason to ask for rider limits, intended use, test standard, paint/hardware exclusions, size of the weighed sample, and independent measurements. “850 g” without those details is a marketing number, not a complete engineering statement.
